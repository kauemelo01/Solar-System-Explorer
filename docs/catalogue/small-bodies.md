# Dwarf planets, asteroids, centaurs and comets

65 named bodies. Moons of dwarf planets and asteroids are on [their own page](moons-small-bodies.md), Pluto's moons on [Moons of Pluto](moons-pluto.md), and the rings of Haumea, Quaoar and Chariklo on the [rings page](rings.md). Orbital elements are osculating heliocentric elements taken from the **JPL Small-Body Database (SBDB)**, except Pluto's (see the note under the dwarf planets); most use epoch JD 2461200.5 (2026-Jun-09 TDB). Comet orbits use the elements of their most recent perihelion passage in the SBDB. Each row links to the live SBDB record, so values can be re-checked at any time. In the app, 56 of these bodies are placed by orbits fitted to JPL Horizons over 1800–2050 AD ([Small-body orbits](../sources.md#small-body-orbits)); the elements here are the SBDB's.

The *Surface image* column says what the app draws on each body: real photos, partial maps, painted illustrations or plain shapes. Details and sources: [Surface maps](../sources.md#surface-maps).

**Pluto** is a dwarf planet, but the app computes its orbit like the planets', from the original version of the JPL *Approximate Positions of the Planets* elements (E. M. Standish, valid 1800–2050 AD; JPL's current web page no longer lists Pluto), not from the SBDB. Checked against JPL Horizons, this places Pluto within 0.01° of its true position from 1900 to 2100 AD. The *a*, *e* and *i* shown are that table's J2000 values; its NAIF ID is 999. Its five moons, including Charon, are listed in [Moons of Pluto](moons-pluto.md).

## Notes on sizes and rotation

Each size and rotation period is the **most recent published measurement**. That is the JPL SBDB value (half its diameter, and its rotation period) unless a body is listed below; the values below come from a newer source, or from the only source where the SBDB lists none. In the app, the object's card shows each of these values with a **source** link to the paper or record it comes from.

**Sizes** other than the SBDB's:

- **Eris:** radius 1,163 km (diameter 2,326 km, stellar occultation; Sicardy et al. 2011, *Nature* 478, [doi:10.1038/nature10550](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10550)).
- **Haumea:** an elongated body with semi-axes 1,161 × 852 × 513 km (Ortiz et al. 2017, *Nature*, [arXiv:2006.03113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03113)). The app draws this shape; the *Radius* column gives the longest semi-axis.
- **Makemake:** radius 715 km (Ortiz et al. 2012, *Nature* 491, [doi:10.1038/nature11597](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11597)).
- **Gonggong:** radius 615 km (diameter 1,230 km; Kiss et al. 2019, *Icarus*, [arXiv:1903.05439](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.05439)).
- **Quaoar:** radius 555 km (diameter 1,110 km, stellar occultation; Braga-Ribas et al. 2013, *ApJ* 773, [doi:10.1088/0004-637X/773/1/26](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/773/1/26)).
- **Sedna:** radius 497.5 km (diameter 995 km; Pál et al. 2012, *A&A* 541, [doi:10.1051/0004-6361/201218874](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201218874)).
- **Orcus:** radius 455 km (diameter 910 km, ALMA; Brown & Butler 2018, *AJ* 156, [doi:10.3847/1538-3881/aad9f2](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/aad9f2)).
- **Chariklo:** equivalent radius 124 ± 9 km from the 2013 stellar occultation (Braga-Ribas et al. 2014, *Nature*; [arXiv:1409.7259](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7259)), used instead of the older radiometric diameter in the SBDB.
- **Dinkinesh:** about 790 m at its widest (Lucy team, [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/dinkinesh/)); the app uses the SBDB's mean radius, 0.3595 km (from Levison et al. 2024).
- **Arrokoth:** radius 9.15 km: a contact binary 36 × 20 × 10 km whose volume, 3,210 ± 650 km³, equals a sphere 18.3 ± 1.2 km across (Spencer et al. 2020, *Science* 367, [arXiv:2004.00727](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00727)), measured by New Horizons. The SBDB lists none. The app draws it as one round body of that volume, not as two lobes (a [limitation of the model](../sources.md#limitations-of-the-model)).
- **Pluto:** radius 1,188.3 km, from its JPL Horizons record (NAIF ID 999); the SBDB lists none.
- **Patroclus:** radius 56.5 km, for Patroclus alone, from its JPL Horizons satellite-solution record (ID 920000617). The SBDB diameter, 140.4 km, is for the unresolved pair of Patroclus and Menoetius.
- **2003 EH1:** radius 2.0 ± 0.2 km (Kasuga & Jewitt 2015, *AJ* 150:152, [arXiv:1509.03361](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.03361)), assuming the albedo typical of comet nuclei (0.04). The SBDB lists none.
- **103P/Hartley 2:** mean radius 0.58 ± 0.02 km, a bi-lobed nucleus 2.33 km long (Thomas et al. 2013, *Icarus* 222, 550, [doi:10.1016/j.icarus.2012.05.034](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.icarus.2012.05.034)), measured by the EPOXI flyby; the SBDB's older diameter is 1.6 km.
- **1998 KY26:** 11 m across (SBDB, from Santana-Ros et al. 2025, *Nature Communications* 16, 8275).
- **Eurybates and Orus:** radii 34.65 km and 30.25 km (surface-equivalent diameters 69.3 ± 1.4 km and 60.5 ± 0.9 km, from shape models scaled to stellar occultations; Mottola et al. 2023, *Planetary Science Journal* 4:18, Table 3, [doi:10.3847/PSJ/acaf79](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/acaf79)). The SBDB's NEOWISE diameters (63.9 and 50.8 km, 2012) are older. Polymele and Leucus keep the SBDB's NEOWISE diameters: Leucus's newer shape model (Mottola et al. 2020) gives maximum dimensions, not a mean size.
- **Braille:** radius 0.64 km, the sphere with the volume of its SBDB dimensions, 2.1 × 1.0 × 1.0 km (from the Deep Space 1 flyby); the SBDB lists no diameter.
- **96P/Machholz 1:** radius 3.2 km (SBDB diameter 6.4 km).
- **Placeholder sizes** (marked *est.*), for bodies with no published size: for an asteroid, the diameter its JPL absolute magnitude H gives with a typical albedo of 0.15 (D = 1329 km ÷ √0.15 × 10^(−H/5)): **Torifune** 0.31 km (H = 18.71) and **Kamoʻoalewa** 0.024 km (H = 24.31). For a comet with no published nucleus size or nucleus magnitude, a radius of 0.5 km: **311P/PANSTARRS**, **C/1861 G1 (Thatcher)**, **342P/SOHO**, **P/2008 Y12 (SOHO)** and **P/1999 J6 (SOHO)**. These are for display only (a [limitation of the model](../sources.md#limitations-of-the-model)); each is replaced when a size is published ([Watchlist](../sources.md#watchlist)).
- **C/2020 F3 (NEOWISE):** no published nucleus size was found in a source we could verify; the app uses a placeholder radius of 2.5 km, marked *est.* (a [limitation of the model](../sources.md#limitations-of-the-model))
- **Comet nuclei** (1P, 2P, 8P, 9P, 19P, 21P, 26P, 55P, 67P, 81P, 109P, Hale-Bopp, Hyakutake): diameters from the SBDB physical parameters.

**Rotation periods** other than the SBDB's:

- **Eris:** 378.9 h (15.79 days, rotating in step with its moon Dysnomia; Szakáts et al. 2023, *A&A* 669, [doi:10.1051/0004-6361/202245234](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202245234)). The SBDB still lists an older 25.9 h (Roe et al. 2008).
- **Quaoar:** 17.68 h (Ortiz et al. 2003; the period used by Morgado et al. 2023, *Nature* 614, [doi:10.1038/s41586-022-05629-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-05629-6), the most recent). The SBDB lists half of it, 8.84 h (Rabinowitz et al. 2007), the single-peaked lightcurve period.
- **67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko:** 12.055 h, the most recent measured period. The app keeps 67P's IAU 2015 spin pole but not its IAU prime meridian, because the IAU rate (12.4043 h) is older; so 67P spins from an arbitrary starting angle (see [Which side faces you](../sources.md#which-side-faces-you)). The most recent measured period is 12.055 h, at the end of the Rosetta mission in September 2016 ([ESA](https://sci.esa.int/web/rosetta/-/58367-comet-rotation-period)). The SBDB lists 12.761 h. 67P's spin changes at each pass near the Sun, because gas escaping from its surface pushes on it. Measured values:
  - 12.761 h before the 2009 perihelion and 12.4043 h after it ([Mottola et al. 2014](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/abs/2014/09/aa24590-14/aa24590-14.html), *A&A* 569). The IAU 2015 report uses 12.4043 h; see NAIF's [`pck00011.tpc`](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/pck/pck00011.tpc).
  - 12.4041 h from July to October 2014 (Jorda et al. 2016, *Icarus*).
  - Slightly longer in spring 2015, then much shorter after the August 2015 perihelion, reaching 12.055 h in 2016 (ESA; [Keller et al. 2015](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/abs/2015/07/aa26421-15/aa26421-15.html), *A&A* 579). Over the 2014–2016 pass the period shortened by about 21 minutes, as on the pass before ([Kramer et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01490), *A&A* 630, A3).
  - 12.055 h is the most recent measured value. 67P passed perihelion again in November 2021, and its period has probably changed since, but we found no later measurement. The app keeps one fixed period, so 67P's spin is only approximate at other dates (a [limitation of the model](../sources.md#limitations-of-the-model)).
- **Donaldjohanson:** 251.09 h (10.46 days), measured from Lucy's 2025 flyby images, from the Lucy project's spin model ([`donaldjohanson_v12.tpc`](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/LUCY/kernels/pck/donaldjohanson_v12.tpc), DLR). The SBDB lists none.
- **1P/Halley:** 52.8 h (2.2 days; Belton et al. 1991, *Icarus* 93, [doi:10.1016/0019-1035(91)90207-A](https://doi.org/10.1016/0019-1035(91)90207-A)). **C/1995 O1 (Hale-Bopp):** 11.4 h (Licandro et al. 1998, *ApJ* 501, [doi:10.1086/311465](https://doi.org/10.1086/311465)). The SBDB lists none for either.
- **19P/Borrelly:** 26.64 h, from its IAU 2015 rotation rate (324.3° a day; NAIF `pck00011`). The SBDB lists none.
- **2003 EH1:** 12.650 ± 0.033 h (Kasuga & Jewitt 2015). The SBDB lists none.
- **Eurybates and Orus:** 8.7027283 h and 13.486190 h, sidereal periods from Mottola et al. 2023 (Table 3). The SBDB's lightcurve-database values (8.711 h and 13.45 h) are older.
- **Leucus:** 445.683 h (18.57 days), sidereal period from Mottola et al. 2020, *Planetary Science Journal* 1:73 ([arXiv:2009.08951](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.08951)). The SBDB lists 445.924 h.
- **9P/Tempel 1:** 40.60 h (212.807° a day), the most recent measured period: the spin rate at the Stardust-NExT flyby in February 2011, as given in the IAU 2015 report (NAIF's [`pck00011.tpc`](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/pck/pck00011.tpc)). Its spin speeds up at each perihelion: 41.335 h before 2000, 41.055 h between 2000 and 2005, 40.827 h in 2006–2010 ([Belton et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.icarus.2011.01.006), *Icarus* 213); it shortened by 13.49 minutes during the 2005 perihelion ([Chesley et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.icarus.2012.03.022), *Icarus* 222). 9P has passed perihelion again in 2016 and 2022, but we found no later measurement, so its spin is only approximate today. The SBDB lists an older 40.7 h. Ceres, Pallas, Lutetia, Eros, Itokawa, Gaspra and Šteins are also spun at their IAU rates, which agree with the SBDB periods within 0.1%.
- **Polymele:** the SBDB's 5.8607 h. Lucy's spin kernel ([`polymele_v11.tpc`](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/LUCY/kernels/pck/polymele_v11.tpc)) uses twice that, 11.514 h, from an unpublished Lucy-team update; the app keeps the SBDB value until it is published.
- **Patroclus:** the SBDB's 102.8 h, the same as Menoetius's orbital period: the pair turn in step.
- **No published period** in a source we could verify: 109P/Swift-Tuttle, 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, 81P/Wild 2, 8P/Tuttle, 21P/Giacobini-Zinner, 26P/Grigg-Skjellerup, 96P/Machholz 1, 311P/PANSTARRS, C/1861 G1 (Thatcher), 342P/SOHO, P/2008 Y12 (SOHO), P/1999 J6 (SOHO), C/1996 B2 (Hyakutake) and C/2020 F3 (NEOWISE). The app spins them once every 12 hours as an illustration only. Periods for Hyakutake (Schleicher et al. 1998, *Icarus* 131) and 8P/Tuttle (Harmon et al. 2010, *Icarus* 207) are published, but we could not check the values in an open source. (A [limitation of the model](../sources.md#limitations-of-the-model).)
